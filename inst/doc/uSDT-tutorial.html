Fitting a hierarchical SDT model

library(uSDT)

What is uSDT for?

uSDT models paired awareness and influence measures from the same participants. It estimates their mean sensitivities, between-subject variation and latent association. The latent regression estimates influence sensitivity at zero awareness sensitivity. Interpreting this as unconscious processing requires a suitable model and an adequate awareness measure.

Prepare the data with usdt_data_tasks(), fit with hsdt(), then inspect the tests, plots and reliability.

The data

The package includes Experiment 2 of Vadillo, Malejka, and Shanks (2025). vadillo_awareness contains old/new recognition judgements. vadillo_cuing contains search response times. Contextual cuing predicts faster responses to repeated (old) displays.

Subject IDs link participants across tasks. Individual trials need not be paired. Column names and condition labels may differ between tasks.

This example uses all supplied trials, including search errors, across blocks and display conditions. It demonstrates the package workflow using the source data. Define exclusions and select the relevant trials before dichotomizing. The package does not remove errors or RT outliers automatically.

Preparing the data

usdt_data_tasks() takes one data frame per task. Give shared arguments once and task-specific arguments as list(awareness = ..., influence = ...).

data_usdt <- usdt_data_tasks(
  awareness = vadillo_awareness,
  influence = vadillo_cuing,
  subject_col      = "subj",
  condition_col    = "condition",
  condition_levels = c(signal = "old", noise = "new"),
  response_col     = list(awareness = "judged.old", influence = "rt"),
  response_levels  = list(
    awareness = c(signal = 1, noise = 0),
    influence = c(signal = "faster", noise = "slower")
  ),
  dichotomize = list(awareness = FALSE, influence = TRUE)
)

The median split is computed within subject, pooling conditions (Meyen et al., 2022). RTs below the median become signal responses. Ties become noise responses by default. A hit is an old display searched faster than the median. A false alarm is a new display searched faster than the median. The resulting \(d'\) measures discrimination by this binary classifier.

With balanced signal/noise trials and an exact half split, the classical criterion is zero under the default deviation coding. Ties, odd trial counts or unequal condition counts can change this. hsdt() fixes a dichotomized task’s criterion to zero when its mean absolute subject criterion is below 0.02. fix_criteria = "none" estimates all criteria.

coding = "deviation" codes signal/noise as \(+0.5/-0.5\), so the probit intercept is \(-c\). Treatment coding uses \(1/0\), giving the intercept \(z(FAR)=-\lambda\), where \(\lambda=c+d'/2\). Both define sensitivity as \(d'=z(HR)-z(FAR)\). They specify different random-effects models when criteria and sensitivities are constrained to independent blocks, so fitted sensitivities need not be identical.

Fixed sensitivity effects are task means. Random effects describe subject departures. sd(d') measures between-subject variation and cor(d') the latent correlation. Subject estimates combine the fixed effects with conditional random-effect modes. Inspect convergence and singularity before interpreting the tests.

The three hypotheses

The default tests are two-sided against zero, without multiplicity adjustment.

  • H1, mean difference: \(\Delta d'=\mu_I-\mu_A\) (influence minus awareness). Here it is -0.11, 95% CI [-0.18, -0.04]. The interval is below zero, favouring awareness sensitivity. An influence advantage would require a positive difference. Its interpretation also depends on how the tasks measure conscious information.
  • H2, latent correlation: \(\rho=\) 0.49, 95% CI [-0.67, 0.95]. This describes the association between latent subject sensitivities. The wide interval provides little precision. A nonsignificant test does not establish independence.
  • H3, latent regression: the slope is 0.36 and the intercept is 0.04, 95% CI [-0.18, 0.27]. The intercept is expected influence sensitivity at latent awareness sensitivity zero, not the mean of participants selected for low observed scores. Its interval includes zero, leaving both zero and positive influence sensitivity compatible with these data.

Writing the awareness variance as \(\tau_A^2\) and the covariance as \(\tau_{AI}\),

\[ E(d'_I\mid d'_A=x)=\beta_0+\beta_1 x,\qquad \beta_1=\tau_{AI}/\tau_A^2,\qquad \beta_0=\mu_I-\beta_1\mu_A. \]

A positive intercept requires an adequate awareness measure and a suitable latent model to support an unconscious-processing interpretation. It may involve extrapolation to zero. The analytical tests of H2 and the H3 slope share a covariance test, so they provide the same test of association.

At \(\alpha=.05\), equivalence and one-sided tests report 90% intervals. Here the sensitivity difference is equivalent within \(\pm0.2\) despite being different from zero. The intercept and correlation tests do not establish their proposed bounds. Correlation tests use the Fisher-\(z\) scale, so their p-values can differ from the covariance test in usdt_tests().

Reliability of individual differences

usdt_reliability() estimates \(\tau^2/(\tau^2+\overline v)\) for each task: between-subject sensitivity variance divided by total variance, with measurement variance \(\overline v\) from binomial-probit information at the fitted probabilities.

reliability <- usdt_reliability(fit_uSDT)

It limits precision for individual differences even when the mean effect is clear. The latent model accounts for trial noise under its assumptions. Visualizing the model

Latent regression and shrinkage

plot(fit_uSDT)

The left panel regresses observed influence \(d'\) on observed awareness \(d'\). Measurement error in the predictor can flatten the slope. The right panel draws the latent regression implied by the fitted covariance, with segments connecting observed pairs to their conditional model estimates. The line is not fitted through those partially pooled points. Its band is a pointwise confidence interval for the regression mean.

Use nrow = 2 to stack the panels and caption = FALSE to hide the caption. The shrinkage plot isolates the movement of the subject estimates:

plot(fit_uSDT, type = "shrinkage")

Subject intervals

plot(fit_uSDT, type = "caterpillar")

Observed intervals use Miller standard errors and a normal approximation. Model intervals include uncertainty in the means, variance components and subject effects when the joint covariance is available. The percentages describe intervals containing zero. They do not classify participants as aware or unaware.

ROC curves

plot(fit_uSDT, type = "roc")

These equal-variance SDT curves describe a subject whose random effects are set to zero. Points mark fitted criteria and bands transform the sensitivity intervals. Binary responses identify one operating point per task. The full curves follow from the model.

plot(fit_uSDT, type = "roc", subject_id = 2040)

Subject curves use partially pooled estimates, with population curves dashed behind them. Their bands condition on the fitted population parameters.

Further analyses

usdt_boot() adds parametric bootstrap inference to a frequentist fit:

fit_boot <- usdt_boot(fit_uSDT, nsim = 1000, ncores = 2, seed = 2026)
summary(fit_boot)
fit_boot$tests

At least 500 usable replicates are required before bootstrap inference replaces the analytical results. Converged boundary refits are retained when all hypothesis estimates are finite. Inspect failures and retained boundary counts in fit_boot$boot. The bootstrap simulates binary responses at the original cell sizes. It does not repeat the RT median split or resolve a lack of information in the design. Read the returned $tests table to retain bootstrap results. usdt_tests() recomputes analytical tests.

Bayesian estimation covers priors, posterior summaries and Bayes factors with hsdt(estimation = "bayesian"). Planning a study covers power, sample sizes and detectable effects. For covariates, see the custom lme4 and brms examples in usdt_tests().
